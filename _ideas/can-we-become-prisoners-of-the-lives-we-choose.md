We build our lives through the decisions we make at different stages of our lives. Our education, careers, relationships, where we live, and daily habits gradually become part of the lives we build. We consider our current circumstances, needs, and expectations for the future when making these decisions. As the years pass, our circumstances and expectations can change. The life we once willingly chose might no longer be the life we want. So why is it so hard to walk away from a life we've built through our own choices?

Perhaps the life we choose becomes the life we cannot leave when the reasons we chose it in the first place are no longer the reasons we stay. What begins as a personal choice might gradually become intertwined with our identity, responsibilities, relationships, and sense of security. At some point, walking away is no longer just about wanting something different. It means questioning everything we have built around that choice. We might still have the freedom to leave; however, exercising that freedom might become increasingly difficult.

We cannot evaluate past decisions independently of the circumstances in which we made them. If a decision no longer suits us today, it doesn't mean it was wrong at the time. A person who prioritised their career in their twenties might want a quieter life in their forties. These two choices don't necessarily contradict each other. When our circumstances and needs change, I don't think we have to stick to the decisions we made in the past. Yet, it isn't easy to walk away from a life we've spent years building. We think about the time we've invested, the sacrifices we've made, and the people who have supported us. Sometimes we even feel that we owe loyalty to our past selves.

I don't think people who have supported us have a say in our future simply because of their support. If someone has helped us, it doesn't mean we have to live our lives according to their expectations. However, our freedom to change our own lives doesn't mean we can ignore the consequences of our decisions for others. For example, someone who wants to change their career may have to consider how their decision might affect their family. Sometimes we put off the change we want to make instead of giving up on it. But postponing it comes at a cost. For some people, the changes we postpone may never happen.

The idea of change itself can be unsettling. We may be afraid of losing the life we're used to, of not having the strength to build a new one, or of finding that the alternative we've chosen isn't as good as we hoped. We may even want something different again after making the change we wanted. Each fresh start brings not only new possibilities but also the uncertainty of starting over. We may not always be ready to pay that price. Sometimes, we choose a familiar life that works over an unfamiliar one that we think might suit us better.

What's even more interesting is that we sometimes stop questioning whether we really want the life we're living. Sometimes we know we're not happy, but we don't want to face what that really means. Asking ourselves certain questions might mean reconsidering decisions we've believed were right for years. Even the comfort of thinking that we could change our lives might be enough for us. We tell ourselves we can build a new life someday, but we keep postponing that day. Over time, the possibility of a new life becomes a thought we consider but never act on.

Natalie, a character in Caro Claire Burke's novel _Yesteryear_, is trapped in the life she has built. She tries to maintain the image of a happy family on social media, even as she faces serious problems in her marriage. She cannot step away from her content schedule, even in the middle of a personal crisis. For Natalie, this identity is not only about how others see her. It is also tied to her work, income, and social status. For this reason, her behaviour cannot be explained by fear of change alone. What interests me about Natalie's story is how the identity she created has gradually begun to shape her life. Perhaps maintaining the life she originally chose has become more important than questioning why she still wants to live that way.

Natalie's circumstances may be unusual, but the questions her story raises are not. When we define ourselves by a particular job, relationship, or way of life, we may face a similar question: Who would we be if one of these were no longer part of our lives? Sometimes we may be more afraid of not knowing how to define ourselves without that life than of walking away from it. And we don't even have to be completely unhappy with our current lives to feel this way. Even being afraid to consider another possibility might reveal how much our choices can limit us.

All of this doesn't mean we should make major changes whenever we're unhappy with our lives. A new life is no guarantee of greater happiness. We might also consciously decide to continue living our current lives after considering our circumstances and responsibilities. But are we really reconsidering that decision, or are we simply following a decision we made years ago without questioning whether it still makes sense?

There is a difference between becoming prisoners of the lives we choose and accepting responsibility for the consequences of our choices. Accepting our responsibilities doesn't mean assuming that another life is impossible. Of course, having other options doesn't necessarily mean we can turn them into reality. Yet questioning the decisions we made in the past can help us reconsider who we are today and how we want to live. Perhaps the reason we cannot walk away from a life is not always that we still want it. Sometimes, it's that we don't know who we would be if we left that life behind.
