One of the things that stayed with me while reading Baek Sehee’s “I Want to Die but I Want to Eat Tteokbokki” was how she kept questioning her own behavior, yet continued to struggle with the same problems. She was able to trace the origins of some of her behaviors to her childhood or earlier experiences. In addition, she managed to see how hard she was criticizing herself. But this level of awareness was not always enough to change her behavior.

Baek talks about low self-esteem, her need for validation from others, and her fear of rejection. In one of the therapy sessions with her therapist, she was confessing that she had been twisting the truth to receive appreciation from other people. She also struggles with conflicting feelings in her relationships. She wants to be independent, yet she is afraid of being alone. After a relationship ends, she feels empty. The contradiction the book is named after is another example of this. Even in her despair, she still wants something as ordinary as a bowl of tteokbokki. I also appreciate that Baek doesn’t try to present herself as better than she is and writes about her struggles while she is still going through them.

Baek also sees her depression as part of who she is. In her second book, “I Want to Die But I Still Want to Eat Tteokbokki”, she talks about how depression and self-pity for herself have become something familiar and safe. This made me wonder whether part of what makes change so difficult for her is the thought of losing something that feels familiar and safe.

Baek mentions her anxiety about what others think about her. She thinks her coworkers don’t like her because she follows working hours and takes annual leave. Besides, she shares her ongoing concerns about how others perceive her appearance with her therapist. Her therapist tries to explain to her how her self-worth and body image are tied to outsiders’ assessments.

It looks like the fear of not being liked by others and the need for others’ validation have dragged her into a vicious cycle. In time, she begins to understand that other people’s judgments have become her inner voice; she cannot escape attaching importance to other people’s criticism. She becomes more aware of this cycle, but she is also struggling to change her behavior.

In her first book, Baek takes a step further in her search to better understand herself and accept who she is. However, she finds herself in the middle of a crisis in her second book. The thoughts of self-harm that she mentioned in her first book take on a more serious dimension in her second book. She reflects on what she has lived without, adding no drama at this stage in her discussion with her therapist. Her therapist tries to understand how Baek feels by asking her detailed questions. I found these pages particularly difficult to read.

At times, the conversations between Baek and her therapist seem to be left unfinished. This sometimes interrupts the flow of the book, but it also gives me the impression that Baek struggles to understand and express her own thoughts. It made me wonder: How can someone express their thoughts clearly when they are still struggling to understand their own thought patterns?

After reading both books, I felt that understanding yourself better doesn’t necessarily mean you can change the way you think or behave. At the end of her first book, Baek mentions that she is beginning to experience little changes. However, at the end of the second book, she is facing uncertainties in both her personal and professional life. I still wonder how much of that kindness toward herself carries over into her everyday life. Still, when she says she no longer hates herself, I see that as a small sign of progress.
