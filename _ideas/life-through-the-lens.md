Taking pictures to keep our memories has become a part of everyday life for many of us. Before social media, photos helped us remember and share memories with a close circle of friends; in the age of social media, we can share those same moments with a much wider audience. Caro Claire Burke's novel Yesteryear has made me rethink the relationship between the pieces we share online and the ones we actually live. First, I reviewed the novel through the lens of tradwife culture and its effects on society. Nevertheless, the more I thought about the book, the more I wondered: Could sharing our lives gradually change the way we live them?

We don't need that many followers or to make money from social media to post our lives online. Reaching for our phones has become a spontaneous act when we travel, discover a new place, or come across a beautiful view. Sometimes we want to have a photo of ourselves at a popular restaurant or a well-known spot, even if we are only going to share it with family or friends. However, what interests me is not just sharing; it is the possibility that we're gradually starting to plan our lives around the photos we want to share. Are we taking photos to remember our experiences, or are we creating experiences to photograph them? 

While thinking about how the images we constantly encounter on social media might influence our preferences, I remembered the concept of the mere-exposure effect (Zajonc, 1968). Under some circumstances, we tend to view things more positively when we see them repeatedly. When we are exposed to the same images of similar places, trips and lifestyles, are the experiences we want to have shaped by them? Although getting inspiration from others is a natural part of our lives, we might be paying more attention to being known for having that experience, as well as to having it, in the age of social media. 

We mostly decide for ourselves which parts of our lives we share on social media. We initially share our experiences, our interests, or our hobbies. Over time, what we post can become part of our online identity; we might even start to be known for a hobby or a habit. The more we emphasise specific parts of our lives, the more we influence how others know us. Others don't even need to expect us to maintain this identity. Being consistent with the identity we've created might affect our behaviours and preferences over time. 

How far can we go to stay aligned with the identity we present to others? For example, let's think about a person who constantly shares posts about books on social media. Over time, he might become known for reading a lot. This situation might motivate him to read more. But what if he starts reading less? Might he still try to appear as someone who reads a lot? If the identity we have built on social media influences our real lives, this doesn't always have to be a negative thing. It might become a problem if our interests or priorities change and we continue living a lifestyle we no longer want, only to maintain our online image.

I think there is an important difference between choosing moments from our lives to share and acting as if we are living a life we don't have, just to maintain our online identity. What we share on social media might reflect specific parts of our lives, but that doesn't mean it isn't real. However, if we consciously show a lifestyle that we don't really have, we might be doing more than just sharing moments from our lives; we might be creating a different reality in others' eyes.

On the other hand, having our online identity entirely based on our real experiences might not make it easier to move away from it. If we have been known for a particular image for a long time, it might take more than changing the way we share to leave this online identity behind. If it has become part of how we define ourselves, letting go of it might make us question who we are and how we want to live now.

Natalie from the novel _Yesteryear_ is a dramatic example who shows us the gap between her real life and the life she presents on social media. The life she presents to her followers consists of carefully selected images and, from time to time, directly contradicts reality. She tries to preserve the happy family image despite problems in her marriage; she continues to follow her content-creation calendar even in the middle of a crisis. She nearly sees herself as a stranger when she comes across the images of her real self. Social media is also a job, a source of income, and an important part of Natalie’s social position. Therefore, it is not possible to explain her efforts to maintain her online identity solely for psychological reasons. Still, her behaviour makes us think that this identity might have become a significant part of how she defines herself. It is not right to treat Natalie’s experiences as equivalent to those of ordinary social media users; however, her story makes me question how the identity we choose to build can limit us over time.

Taking pictures, sharing our experiences, or getting inspiration from others’ lives are not problematic behaviours on their own. However, when our urge to stay consistent with our social media identity starts to influence our preferences and behaviours, the boundary between the life we want to live and the life we show might become blurred. On top of that, being aware of this change doesn’t mean we can change it. Maybe the real problem is not the life we show others, but how far we can move away from it when we no longer want to live it.
